Introduction

An individual’s identity is a fundamental human right, guaranteed by international conventions and domestic legislation. In the case of Milivoj Miletić, Lakatoš on his father’s side (officially and illegally Milan Petrović), his right to his true identity has been jeopardized through illegal adoption and forced assimilation into a Serbian ethnic identity, which has no genetic, historical or cultural basis in his case.

Genetic analyses, historical information and legal-administrative facts confirm the true ethnic origin and cultural affiliation that were systematically falsified and erased for political and legal reasons. This process began after the probate hearing, when it was stated that the late Pal Lakatoš had another heir: the then five-year-old child Milivoj Miletić, whom Pal cared for until his death and acknowledged as his son, something subsequently proved through indirect genetic markers. From that moment, a legal and administrative circus began, aimed at erasing the child’s identity and preventing him from exercising inheritance, personal and national rights, including the right to ethnic affiliation, the language he spoke — Hungarian — and every other aspect of his cultural and family heritage.

1. Genealogical Context and the Question of Assimilation

Milivoj Miletić is the son of Cvita Miletić (a Croatian woman from Dalmatia) and Pavle (Pal) Lakatoš (a Hungarian). His origin is unequivocally Croatian-Hungarian, but through illegal adoption and administrative manipulation he was forcibly assimilated into a Serbian identity.

Key legal and historical arguments:

  • His biological family was never part of the Serbian ethnic community, meaning that the change of ethnic identity was forced and carried out without a basis.
  • His adoption and change of name served as a tool for erasing his true origin.

  • The process is part of a wider pattern of forced assimilation, documented in other cases of illegal adoption in the former Yugoslavia and across Europe.
  • An important comparison: in history, Magyarization was often voluntary, whereas this case involves forced assimilation, similar to the devshirme system in the Ottoman Empire, in which children were forcibly separated from their ethnic and cultural roots.
  • Conclusion: this is not an ordinary adoption case, but an example of ethnic erasure through legal manipulation.

2. Genetic Analysis of Origin

DNA analysis performed through MyHeritage, 23andMe and Y-STR analyses unequivocally confirms the origin:

2.1. Paternal Line (Y-DNA) — Haplogroup I-S17250

  • It belongs to I2a1a2 (the northern variant of I2), which is dominant among Central European peoples.
  • This haplogroup is NOT characteristic of ethnic Hungarians of Finno-Ugric origin, meaning that the Lakatoš family was probably part of Central European populations subsequently assimilated into Hungarian culture.
  • The highest concentration of this haplogroup is found in:
    • Northern Croatia;
    • Vojvodina;
    • Hungary;
    • Slovakia and the Czech Republic;
    • Not among southern Balkan Serbs.
  • Conclusion: Milivoj Miletić is not part of a Serbian male line, but belongs to Central European peoples, with later assimilation into a Hungarian environment.

2.2. Maternal Line (mtDNA) — Haplogroup HV6a

  • Present in Dalmatia, Italy, Greece and the Middle East.
  • Not typical of Slavic peoples of the Balkans, but dominant among Dalmatians, Illyrians and Mediterranean populations.
  • Confirms the family information that the author’s mother is a Croatian woman from Dalmatia.
  • Conclusion: the maternal line shows predominantly Mediterranean and Dalmatian roots, wholly incompatible with the administratively imposed “Serbian” identity.

3. Ethnicity Estimate through Autosomal DNA

Genetic analyses show the following percentages:

  • 52.4% Balkan origin (Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia) — predominantly from Dalmatia and Pannonia.
  • 31.4% Eastern European origin (Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, Czech Republic) — confirms Central European roots within Hungary.
  • 9.4% Greek-Albanian origin — possible Dalmatian or Vlach traces.
  • 4.1% Baltic origin — characteristic of Slavs.
  • 2.7% Germanic origin — probably Austrian-German influence in Pannonia.

Conclusion

DNA analysis clearly shows that Milivoj Miletić is NOT genetically Serbian, but belongs to Central European peoples, predominantly of Croatian-Hungarian origin.

4. Historical Context of Assimilation

  • During the Habsburg Monarchy and Yugoslavia, there were cases of voluntary Magyarization, but not violent changes of identity through adoption, which makes this case distinctive.
  • Milivoj Miletić’s illegal adoption was not the result of natural assimilation, but of a forced change of ethnic identity through administrative manipulation.
  • His identity was administratively falsified, and his right to biological belonging was taken away.

Conclusion and Further Steps

  • Milivoj Miletić’s illegal adoption was a legal act of forced assimilation, rather than a genuine adoption process in the child’s interest.
  • DNA analysis confirms his non-Serbian origin and denies any basis for ethnic membership of the Serbian community.
  • Further legal steps include:
    • Court proceedings to restore identity;
    • Approaching international human-rights institutions;
    • International media pressure to expose the systemic abuse of such cases publicly.

Closing sentence

The truth has now been clearly proved — Milivoj Miletić is NOT a Serb, but Hungarian and Croatian, whose identity was forcibly changed through administrative manipulation.

Editorial provenance note

The six-page source is the author’s undated analysis, incorporating a DNA-matching screenshot and an “Ancient Origins” graphic. It is not an original STR laboratory report and contains no STR marker table. The email of 3 March 2025 names an attachment with the same report title. Acknowledgment of receipt of that message does not establish that the attachment was opened. The analysis itself supplies no composition or submission date.

Later author’s clarification — known family relationships and personal contact

The author explains that the same DNA profile shows matches with the known paternal Lakatos branch and the maternal Kučić and Kamber branches, rather than merely with a person bearing the surname Lakatos. His mother’s mother’s maiden surname was Kamber. The Kučić relative shown is the son of the author’s maternal uncle’s daughter: a man surnamed Kučić married that daughter, and the displayed match is their son. Here, maternal uncle means the author’s mother’s brother, not his father’s brother. The author states that he has also met some of the relatives shown in person, without specifying here which of them he met. This is his later explanation of the family relationships; the report’s original wording and the platform’s displayed probable-relationship labels remain separate.
