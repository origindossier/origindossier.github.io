PLAINTIFF’S SUBMISSION

(In response to defendant’s submission no. 560-111-1/2024 of 22 December 2025; supplementation of proposed evidence and legal reasoning)

I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL FRAMEWORK

By submission no. 560-111-1/2024 of 22 December 2025, the defendant supplies certain archive documents (the dossier cover, foster-carer questionnaire, contracts and decisions on family placement, case-monitoring sheet and beneficiary’s personal record), and draws the following conclusions from those documents:

  • that, as a child, I was “without parental care”;
  • that allegedly “none of the close relatives” wanted to assume care of me;
  • that adoption was “the best form of protection under family law”;
  • that “there were no grounds for annulling the adoption at the time it was established” and “everything complied with the regulations then in force”.

At the same time, the defendant has still not complied with all this Court’s orders, particularly those requiring submission of:

  • the complete documentation concerning the acquisition of authority over the case (“how the child came under its authority”); and
  • the complete file on the full adoption in 1979,

in accordance with:

  1. the minutes and orders from the hearing of 26 March 2025; and
  2. the Court’s decision at the hearing of 26 November 2025 (the fifteen- and thirty-day periods).

Through this submission, I:

  1. respond to the assertions in the defendant’s submission of 22 December 2025;
  2. point out the selective and incorrect interpretation of the documentation supplied;
  3. specify the nature of the violation — deprivation of identity, forced assimilation (Serbianization), and the fact that this is a continuing act;
  4. propose that, because of the persistent failure to supply crucial acts, the Court draw adverse evidentiary inferences regarding the disputed points.

Note: This submission is presented as a procedural act in these proceedings, but was also written to reconstruct the facts and institutional conduct comprehensively through evidence, bearing in mind that the consequences of the contested act constitute a continuing violation and that the contents of the judgment and case file will matter beyond a single courtroom.

II. PROBATE PROCEEDINGS O.196/79 — DISPOSAL OF IDENTITY AND INHERITANCE STATUS (MILIVOJ → “MILAN”) AND THE AUTHORITY OF THE GUARDIANSHIP BODY

In probate case O.196/79 before the Municipal Court in Novi Sad, concerning the estate of the late Lakatoš Pavle, the initial decision of 23 November 1979 declared me the statutory heir to one quarter of the estate under the identity Miletić Milivoj, born on 28 June 1974 to mother Cvita Miletić, with the father’s field blank in the birth register and without any subsequent entries. The Court thereby accepted my capacity to inherit in the first order of succession, at least as a potential son of the deceased born outside marriage.

However, the remaining heirs’ representative, lawyer Pervaz Jovan, filed an appeal with the Higher Court in Novi Sad (Gž 11/80), stating, among other things, that:

“Proceedings for the full adoption of Milivoj by another family were conducted before the Self-Managing Community of Interest for Social Welfare, during which the adoptive parents stated that they did not seek for Milivoj to inherit from anyone, but for him to be regarded as their child.”

A lawyer acting for private heirs could not have obtained such information from publicly accessible sources, but only:

  • from the files of the then Self-Managing Community of Interest / guardianship body; or
  • through direct communication with that body.

This means that, even then, the guardianship body was actively communicating the contents of “my” status in full-adoption proceedings to third parties — the heirs and their lawyer — to protect their property interests, rather than to protect the child, and to the detriment of that child, namely me.

In plain terms: my identity and inheritance status were treated as subjects of an agreement concerning the distribution of property, rather than as a child’s rights that the body was supposed to protect.

At the same time:

  • I appear in the probate proceedings as Miletić Milivoj, a potential heir of Pavle Lakatoš (one quarter of the estate), while
  • in the files of the Self-Managing Community of Interest / Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad, the same person is processed as a child “without parental care”, intended for full adoption into another family, with the proposition that “the adoptive parents do not seek for the child to inherit from anyone”.

The law does not recognize a situation in which one child simultaneously:

  • exists as a potential son and heir in probate proceedings; and
  • is treated through another channel as entirely cut off from any inheritance, through a scenario of full adoption into another family.

This is not an ordinary “procedural error”, but a conscious disposal of the child’s identity and inheritance status, in which the guardianship body effectively assumes the role of “managing” whether

an heir named Milivoj (Lakatoš) will exist or will be “transformed” into Milan (Vasić / Petrović), without any real opportunity for me, as a child, to protect my right to identity and inheritance.

Supplement: internal contradiction concerning the guardianship body

As crucial evidence of irregularities concerning the guardianship body’s authority, I single out the letter from the Municipal Court in Novi Sad, case no. O.196/79, dated 20 July 1979, addressed to:

“THE SECRETARIAT FOR VETERANS’ AND DISABLED VETERANS’ AFFAIRS AND SOCIAL PROTECTION — THE GUARDIANSHIP BODY — NOVI SAD”,

in which the Court requests that:

“A guardian be appointed for minor Milivoj, the deceased’s son born outside marriage, to protect his rights and interests in the probate proceedings.”

This document is significant in several respects:

  1. The Court explicitly identifies the Secretariat as the guardianship body. Thus, in July 1979, official judicial correspondence identifies precisely the Secretariat as the competent guardianship body for minor Miletić Milivoj.
  2. In contrast, the “minutes of full adoption” of 21 June 1979 were drawn up by the Self-Managing Community of Interest for Social Protection in Novi Sad, whose preamble invokes a municipal assembly decision from 1977 and describes itself as the “authorized guardianship body”.

This creates an absurd situation:

  • one body, the Self-Managing Community of Interest, declares itself the guardianship body and decides on the child’s full adoption; while
  • another body, the Secretariat, serves the Court one month later as the sole guardianship body to which it turns for the appointment of a guardian for the same child in probate proceedings.

Even if it were accepted that a certain scope of duties had been “entrusted” to the Self-Managing Community of Interest, such entrustment cannot derogate from the statutory provision identifying the guardianship body, nor turn void administrative acts into a lawful basis for full adoption.

A municipal assembly decision cannot change authority established by statute, much less justify recording the same child:

  • as the deceased’s son born outside marriage, for whom the Court seeks a guardian to protect his rights in the probate hearing; and
  • simultaneously as a child over whom full adoption was supposedly established as early as 21 June 1979 before another body calling itself the “guardianship body”.

In practice, this means that, at the time the Court officially proceeds on the basis that I am Miletić Milivoj, the deceased’s son born outside marriage whose inheritance rights need protection, the Self-Managing Community of Interest / Centre for Social Work simultaneously conducts proceedings in which I am treated as a child “without identity” and “without capacity to inherit”, prepared for full adoption with the claim that the adoptive parents “do not seek for the child to inherit from anyone”.

III. “OWNERSHIP” OF IDENTITY AND FORCED ADMINISTRATIVE ASSIMILATION — EFFECTIVE “SERBIANIZATION” OF THE IDENTITY OF A CHILD OF HUNGARIAN–CROATIAN ORIGIN — A CONTINUING ACT

The documents cited reveal not merely a social picture, but a clear pattern of interference with the national, family and personal identity of a child of Hungarian–Croatian origin:

  • father: Pal (Pavle) Lakatoš, a Hungarian, with paternity acknowledged in civil case P-1642/76 and capacity to inherit recognized in probate case O.196/79;
  • mother: Cvita Miletić, a Croatian, as recorded in KJ-8 / birth-register entry 2512/1974;
  • adoptive parents in 1979: Milorad and Gospava Vasić, of Serbian nationality;
  • adoptive parents in 1984: Periša and Gorgina Petrović, also of Serbian nationality.

Despite this, the guardianship body:

  • asserts in its internal notes that Pal Lakatoš “claims to be the biological father” and that this is “unlikely”, although judicial files now exist that unequivocally confirm paternity;
  • arrogates to itself the right effectively to nullify acknowledged paternity and paternal Hungarian origin, invoking a social worker’s “assessment” rather than law and facts;
  • rapidly carries out the child’s full adoption by a couple of Serbian nationality, changing the name and civil-register entry so that the adoptive parents appear to be biological parents.

In effect, the Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad behaves as though it can dispose of my identity as property:

  • it changes my first name and surname;
  • it crosses out my paternal origin (Lakatoš, Hungarian) and maternal origin (Miletić, Croatian);
  • it administratively repackages me into an entirely new identity aligned with that of the adoptive parents (Serbian);
  • it does all this when, as a child, I had already developed the awareness that I was Palika — the son of Pal Lakatoš.

Such conduct cannot be regarded as “neutral social protection”, but as forced administrative assimilation (Serbianization): the systematic conversion of a child of a different origin into the majority identity, with the erasure of traces in civil registers and family records.

I particularly emphasize that this violation did not end in 1979. It was:

In reality, as I now clearly know and as the family context shows, I was Palika — “little Pal”, the son of Pal (Pavle) Lakatoš. That was my experienced name and identity, consistent with my paternal line.

  • a misogynistic approach — hatred of or prejudice against women; and
  • a chauvinistic, anti-Croatian attitude towards the mother of a child of another nationality,

which is directly relevant to this case, because today’s Centre for Social Work defends that discourse as lawful and professional.

In the case-monitoring sheet and accompanying notes, Vera Radovanović uses expressions such as:

  • “mother prone to prostitution”;
  • “devoted to prostitution”;
  • “lived with several men”, “had intercourse with several men”.

Nowhere is there reference to:

Instead, female sexuality is criminalized and used as a basis for:

Had this been a man who “lives with several women”, he would most likely have been described as a “bohemian”. When a woman is concerned, the same pattern of behaviour labels her “moral refuse”.

This is not a neutral professional description, but a gender-biased, misogynistic characterization, ultimately legally untenable as a criterion for removing a child.

Vera Radovanović uses the terms:

  • “wandering”;
  • “alcohol”;

Poverty is thus treated not as a social problem, but as a character defect, contrary to the purpose of the guardianship body.

5. The irony and hypocrisy of today’s society and the mother’s actual life

I particularly emphasize the irony and hypocrisy of the way the characterizations directed at my mother are indirectly defended even today. Everything held against a poor Croatian woman in 1979 — “wandering”, “immoral life”, “debauchery”, “alcohol” — no longer constitutes grounds for removing a child in today’s society, but forms the foundation of an entire segment of the public scene euphemistically called “show business”, “starlets” and “influencers”. Moreover, in my mother’s particular case, those labels were not even real, but fabricated.

I took the trouble to investigate her history up to 1972, before she came to the Socialist Republic of Serbia: she had worked as a permanently employed worker, had her own apartment in the very centre of Šibenik and led an orderly life; she went to Vojvodina because of the reassuring words of the man she loved — my father.

The issue, therefore, was and remains not “morality”, but whose identical or similar patterns of behaviour are tolerated and monetized, and whose behaviour, as in my mother’s case, is used as an excuse to remove a child and forcibly assimilate that child.

Evidence of my mother’s actual life is not hypothetical. Living contemporaries in the Republic of Croatia can directly testify about her work, housing status and way of life before she left for Vojvodina — people who knew her as an orderly worker with permanent employment and her own apartment in the centre of Šibenik, rather than as someone fitting the misogynistic stereotypes that the Centre produced on paper.

However, those witnesses will be called upon at the stage and before the forums where there is a real willingness to hear the whole truth — whether in further proceedings or before international institutions and researchers dealing with forced assimilation and abuses of adoption.

If this Court decides to disregard or relativize the fabricated labels that the Centre attributed to my mother, that will be its decision and its risk. But the truth about her actual life will not disappear — it will be presented where evidence is not suppressed to “protect the reputation of institutions”, but studied as an example of what a state must never do again.

Vera Radovanović, whose discourse was already formed in 1979, continued to work as part of that system.

  2. The same lawyer confirms the father’s identity:

    “…it has not been judicially established that the deceased is the father, but he acknowledged him…”

    Thus, the family and the Court know that the father acknowledged the child.

  3. The Court instructs the Centre:

    “Therefore… appointment of a guardian to represent the interests is to be obtained from the Centre for Social Work.”

VI. DOUBLE ADOPTION, DISREGARD OF THE NEGATIVE 1984 ASSESSMENT, AND FALSE “PATERNAL CONSENT”

On several occasions, including the earlier submission of 27 November 2025, the defendant supplies the file on incomplete adoption no. UP 02-987/84 concerning Periša and Gorgina Petrović, while stating that it considers that file “irrelevant”, because formally this action does not seek annulment of that adoption.

However, the very facts:

  • that full adoption by Vasić Milorad and Gospava was already established in 1979, which the defendant does not dispute; and
  • that, in 1984, the same body — the Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad — participated in a second adoption by different adoptive parents,

are directly contrary to Article 7 of the Adoption Act of the Socialist Autonomous Province of Vojvodina then in force (1976), which provides that multiple persons cannot adopt one person unless they are married to one another. An already established full adoption cannot be “transferred” to a new married couple — that is precisely why it is called full adoption.

Thus, the 1984 incomplete-adoption file is not irrelevant: it is evidence of the continuity of the defendant’s unlawful conduct:

  • first, full adoption in 1979 before the fifth birthday, without fulfilling the conditions under Article 30 — without the mother’s consent, effectively disregarding Pal Lakatoš’s paternity and probate proceedings O.196/79;
  • then “readoption” in 1984 by different adoptive parents, although full adoption already existed and would, under the law, exclude another round of adoption.

This is compounded by another crucial element from the 1984 files:

“In my opinion, the application of this married couple, whose behaviour and motivation to adopt a child are appropriate, should not be accepted, because they are at an age that does not promise what an adopted child needs. I recommend that this noble, childless family be advised to accept a child as a foster family.”

Thus:

  • the professional — the psychologist — explicitly recommends not accepting the adoption application;
  • gives the reasons: their age and inability to provide what an adopted child needs;
  • even proposes a less extensive form — foster care rather than adoption.

The assessment also very clearly describes the motivation of married couple Periša and Gorgina Petrović: they want a male child who, as explicitly stated, “when he grows up, would help her and her husband bear the burden of their later years more easily”.

This description of their motives shows that the child is not regarded primarily as a rights-bearing person who should receive redress for the harm already inflicted and a stable emotional and family setting, but as a means — a future helper and labour force in the adoptive parents’ old age.

That pattern also materialized in my later life: during the 1987 school holidays, precisely on my birthday, 28 June, and thereafter until the end of August, I worked in a cold-storage facility instead of enjoying protection as a child and the right to childhood. This is not an isolated episode, but a logical continuation of the utilitarian picture of the child already recorded in the 1984 assessment — the child as someone supposed to “help” adults, rather than someone to whom the system owes protection and treatment of the trauma caused by the previous unlawful proceedings.

Despite the clear negative assessment:

  • the Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad, as guardianship body and proposer,
  • disregards the negative professional assessment by the psychologist of the Centre for Social Work in Kragujevac, contained in the 1984 adoption file;
  • continues the adoption proceedings;
  • and carries them through to completion, although full adoption by the first married couple had already been established in 1979 and although the law does not permit such a course.

There is a further, particularly grave level of legal and factual manipulation here:

In the minutes of the Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad dated 12 September 1984, containing Milorad Vasić’s statement on “consent to the adoption of minor Vasić Milan”, the first adoptive parent is practically treated as a “father” giving consent to adoption, although under the law then in force he was merely the adoptive parent from an already completed full adoption, not a biological parent.

Thus, the Centre:

  • thereby creates the appearance of a “first”, regular adoption, as though the child had no previous adoptive parents;

whereas, in reality:

  • the law prohibits double adoption — Article 7;
  • the previous full adoption of 1979 already exists and produces effects.

In this way:

  • the second adoptive parents, Periša and Gorgina Petrović, are deceived: they are given the impression that they are receiving a child with valid consent from the “old father”, instead of a precise legal explanation that the child is already fully adopted by the first married couple and that the law does not recognize “readoption”;
