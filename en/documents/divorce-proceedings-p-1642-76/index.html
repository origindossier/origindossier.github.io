Divorce proceedings P-1642/76 - court-confirmed paternal-line record

Central court-confirmed paternal-line record. The file identifies Lakatoš Pavle as Hungarian and repeatedly links Pavle / Pal Lakatoš, Cvita Miletić and their child/son through witness testimony, party statements, Pavle’s own statement, Kopljan Roža’s care statement and the court’s factual findings.

Municipal Court in Novi Sad 1977-10-26
Forensic function: P-1642/76 is not merely a divorce document. It is the pre-substitution court record of the paternal line. Bicok Margita, Gabrić Nikola, Kopljan Roža, Lakatoš Vera, Pavle himself and the judgment preserve the Pavle–Cvita–child/son structure. The court’s established factual state records that Pavle lived with Miletić Cvita, fathered a child with her, and that this child was cared for by Kopljan Roža. That court-accepted structure collides directly with the later administrative identity substitution.

The first assertion is factually incorrect and directly contradicts judicial file P-1642/76. We again stress that determining paternity is not the subject of these proceedings. Nevertheless, the Court of Appeal plainly distorted the facts in the case file when its reasoning stated that Pavle Lakatoš had not acknowledged the child.

In divorce proceedings before the District Court in Novi Sad, P-1642/76, at the main hearing of 26 October 1977, Pavle Lakatoš, examined as a party, stated that after leaving hospital he began living with another woman, an agricultural worker, and that he “had a son with her”; he then identified that woman as Cvita.

This fact did not remain merely an oral statement by him.

In its judgment P-1642/76 of 26 October 1977, the District Court in Novi Sad stated in its factual findings that Pavle Lakatoš began living with the worker Miletić Cvita, with whom he had a child.

Accordingly, the Court of Appeal could not truthfully write that Pavle Lakatoš “did not acknowledge paternity”.

If it considered this legally relevant, it could have stated that paternity had not been established by a separate decision.

That is not, however, the same as asserting that Pavle Lakatoš had not acknowledged that the child was his.

Despite the express ground of appeal that Pavle Lakatoš acknowledged the child in the divorce context, the Court of Appeal asserted the opposite in the contested judgment, further distorting the actual content of the appeal and the facts.

The plaintiff did not argue that a final decision had established him as Pavle Lakatoš’s heir.

He argued that the probate files, divorce file P-1642/76 and other judicial documents show that the administrative formula “father unknown” was not a neutral description of an absence of information, but a reduction of the real paternal and inheritance trail existing in state files and directly relevant to the lawfulness of full adoption.

The Court of Appeal avoided this legal problem on two levels:

  • first, it made the factually incorrect assertion that Pavle Lakatoš had not acknowledged paternity;
  • then, it replaced the question of the lawfulness of the administrative classification “father unknown” with the question whether paternity had already been established by a final decision at that time.

These are different questions.

In doing so, the Court of Appeal did not assess the ground of appeal as actually made. It first distorted it factually, then recast it into a weaker version, and then rejected it.

In other words, the first-instance court formally cited Article 30 but did not establish the decisive facts without which that provision cannot be applied: neither the legal framework governing this particular case, nor the duration and course of the search, nor a lawful basis for substituting the special-purpose guardian’s consent for that of a known mother.

3. Pavle Lakatoš and the traces of paternity were not properly assessed

The court reduced the issue of Pavle Lakatoš to the simple observation that paternity had not been judicially established. This bypassed the substance of the matter.

The proceedings raised and documented traces showing that Pavle Lakatoš:

  • acknowledged the child in the context of divorce proceedings;
  • that the family knew of the child’s existence;
  • that I lived within my paternal family circle;
  • that I was staying with my paternal grandmother and grandfather;
  • that my status as the deceased’s son born outside marriage was discussed in the probate proceedings;
  • that at one point I was even declared an heir.

The court did not assess the significance of these traces for whether the administrative formula “father unknown” reflected the actual facts, or represented an administrative reduction of a more complex family and inheritance reality to facilitate full adoption.

4. Probate proceedings O.196/79 were not properly assessed

The probate proceedings were not incidental background, but one of the central pieces of evidence.

They contain:

  • the record of 19 March 1979, stating that the minor Milivoj was staying with his grandparents, namely the deceased’s parents;
  • the letter of 20 July 1979, requesting the appointment of a guardian to protect his rights and interests in the probate proceedings;
  • appellate proceedings Gž 11/80, showing that private heirs possessed information that full-adoption proceedings had taken place before SIZ and that the adopters had stated they did not seek for Milivoj to inherit from anyone.

The court did not explain how the same child could exist in one legal channel as Miletić Milivoj, a potential heir of Pavle Lakatoš, and in another as a child without parental care, ready for full adoption and the rewriting of his identity into another family.

Nor did the judgment explain why it did not consider that material decisive, although it directly concerns identity, inheritance status and the timing of institutional action towards the child.

5. The second adoption in 1984 was left without legal explanation

The judgment states that I was adopted by the Petrović couple in 1984 and was assigned their surname, but does not clarify how this was possible at all if a full adoption had already been lawfully established in 1979.

III. “OWNERSHIP” OF IDENTITY AND FORCED ADMINISTRATIVE ASSIMILATION — EFFECTIVE “SERBIANIZATION” OF THE IDENTITY OF A CHILD OF HUNGARIAN–CROATIAN ORIGIN — A CONTINUING ACT

The documents cited reveal not merely a social picture, but a clear pattern of interference with the national, family and personal identity of a child of Hungarian–Croatian origin:

  • father: Pal (Pavle) Lakatoš, a Hungarian, with paternity acknowledged in civil case P-1642/76 and capacity to inherit recognized in probate case O.196/79;
  • mother: Cvita Miletić, a Croatian, as recorded in KJ-8 / birth-register entry 2512/1974;
  • adoptive parents in 1979: Milorad and Gospava Vasić, of Serbian nationality;
  • adoptive parents in 1984: Periša and Gorgina Petrović, also of Serbian nationality.

Despite this, the guardianship body:

  • asserts in its internal notes that Pal Lakatoš “claims to be the biological father” and that this is “unlikely”, although judicial files now exist that unequivocally confirm paternity;
  • arrogates to itself the right effectively to nullify acknowledged paternity and paternal Hungarian origin, invoking a social worker’s “assessment” rather than law and facts;
  • rapidly carries out the child’s full adoption by a couple of Serbian nationality, changing the name and civil-register entry so that the adoptive parents appear to be biological parents.

In effect, the Centre for Social Work in Novi Sad behaves as though it can dispose of my identity as property:

  • it changes my first name and surname;
  • it crosses out my paternal origin (Lakatoš, Hungarian) and maternal origin (Miletić, Croatian);
  • it administratively repackages me into an entirely new identity aligned with that of the adoptive parents (Serbian);
  • it does all this when, as a child, I had already developed the awareness that I was Palika — the son of Pal Lakatoš.

Such conduct cannot be regarded as “neutral social protection”, but as forced administrative assimilation (Serbianization): the systematic conversion of a child of a different origin into the majority identity, with the erasure of traces in civil registers and family records.
